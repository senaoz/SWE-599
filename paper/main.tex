\title{Does LLM Reranking Help Scientific Paper Recommendation?}
\subtitle{Evidence from Citation and Human Expertise Labels}

\author{Sena Oz}
\affiliation{%
  \institution{Boğaziçi University}
  \city{Istanbul}
  \country{Türkiye}}

\begin{abstract}
We find that small LLM rerankers are sensitive to candidate order in a model-dependent direction, and that under controlled order no LLM reranker, second stage or citation-graph signal we tested beats a dense embedding model on both citation-derived and human expertise labels.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{scientific paper recommendation, LLM reranking, position bias, evaluation}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Task and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
Given a query, each method ranks its candidates so that relevant papers come first.
We compare two lexical methods, BM25~\citep{robertson2009bm25} and TF-IDF; four dense encoders scored by cosine similarity, MiniLM (all-MiniLM-L6-v2)~\citep{wang2020minilm,reimers2019sbert}, SPECTER2 with [CLS] pooling~\citep{singh2023scirepeval,cohan2020specter}, EmbeddingGemma~\citep{vera2025embeddinggemma} and Qwen3-Embedding-8B~\citep{zhang2025qwen3embedding}; the cross-encoder bge-reranker-base~\citep{xiao2024cpack}; Llama~3.2 3B Instruct~\citep{grattafiori2024llama3} used listwise over the full list, listwise with a sliding window of 10 candidates and step 5~\citep{sun2023rankgpt}, and pointwise; and three combinations, a two-stage cascade in which Llama reranks Qwen3-Embedding's top 10 and two score fusions (Llama + MiniLM, Qwen3 + MiniLM).
To test scale, we repeat the listwise experiments with Llama~3.1 8B Instruct, and in the full-corpus and human-label settings we add Qwen3-Reranker-4B~\citep{zhang2025qwen3embedding}, a model trained for reranking.

All models run locally: the Llama models and Qwen3-Embedding as 4-bit (Q4\_K\_M) GGUF files in llama.cpp, the others in sentence-transformers or transformers.
Every method receives the same text, the title, abstract and OpenAlex concepts of each paper, truncated to 600 characters per candidate and 1,500 for the query (chosen on the dev split).
LLMs decode greedily.
The listwise prompt numbers the candidates and asks for a JSON array of their numbers; candidates the model leaves out are appended in random order, and an unparsable answer falls back to a random order, never to the input order.
The pointwise prompt asks for a 0--10 score, which we read as the expected value over the first generated token's probabilities.

\subsection{Citation Labels}
\label{sec:citation-labels}
The queries are 174 papers from OpenAlex published after 2020 by seven institutions (MIT, Stanford, Harvard, UC Berkeley, the University of Michigan, Google and Google DeepMind), each citing 6--14 works; 168 have the required metadata, and a seeded split assigns 50 to dev and 118 to test.
A query's positives are the references it cites that have a title and an abstract (3--14 per query).
Its $2n$ negatives, for $n$ positives, are drawn from the other queries' references published before the query and sharing no author with it, either uniformly (\emph{random}) or among papers that share one of the query's top-5 OpenAlex concepts (\emph{concept}).
The concept condition is harder but matches on field rather than topic: 97.7\% of its negatives share a concept with the query, more than the positives do (73.5\%), but 68.7\% share only a top-level field such as ``Medicine''.
Candidates are shuffled per query and seed, and the order shown to each method is stored.
Because MRR saturates near 1 for strong methods, R-precision is the primary metric; a random ranking scores 33.3\%.

The full-corpus setting ranks each query against 11,967 works: every eligible positive plus every work cited by at least five earlier papers that an OpenAlex title search returned for any query.
These citing papers also form the co-citation graph used in Section~\ref{sec:rq4}, built under five leakage rules, each covered by a unit test: a query never contributes its own references, query duplicates and earlier versions are removed, only citing papers dated before the query count (with year-only dates moved to year end), and no other query paper acts as a citing paper.

\subsection{Human Expertise Labels}
\label{sec:human-labels}
In the gold-standard dataset of \citet{stelmakh2025gold}, 58 researchers rated their expertise in reviewing 477 papers they had read, on a 1--5 scale, 3--10 papers each.
Each researcher is represented by up to 20 of their own papers, in ten random profile samples.
Lexical and dense methods score a rated paper by its maximum similarity to the profile papers, as our deployed system does; Qwen3-Reranker-4B uses each profile paper as a query and also takes the maximum; the LLMs see the profile titles and the candidate, with prompts that keep the citation-benchmark structure but ask about reviewing expertise.
We report the authors' weighted Kendall-$\tau$ loss, for which lower is better and a constant ranking scores 0.5, averaged over profile samples; our evaluation reproduces their published baselines to two decimals.
This setting ranks papers for one researcher, the reverse of our system's direction, and covers a subset of the methods: the three encoders other than EmbeddingGemma, both lexical methods, listwise and pointwise Llama, and Qwen3-Reranker-4B.

\subsection{Statistics}
\label{sec:stats}
We report 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals over queries or researchers and compare methods with paired two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, Holm-corrected~\citep{holm1979simple} within each pre-specified family.
On citation labels, order-sensitive LLM methods are averaged over five shuffle seeds.
The bibliographic-coupling analysis on human labels was pre-registered, the LLM design on human labels was fixed in code before any LLM loss was computed, and the fusion analyses on human labels are post-hoc; we mark them as such.

\section{RQ1: Candidate Order Changes the Answer}
\label{sec:rq1}
Reversing a candidate list that starts with the cited papers, with nothing else changed, lowers the 3B model's R-precision from 72.3 to 30.0 but raises the 8B model's from 73.9 to 89.4, so order sensitivity persists at the larger size but changes direction.

We show each listwise model the same candidates in three orders: cited papers first, the order of our first evaluation; shuffled; and the reverse of the first order, so that the cited papers come last.
Prompt, text and decoding are those of Section~\ref{sec:methods}; the comparison uses the 118 test queries of both benchmarks and one shuffle seed.
Table~\ref{tab:order} gives the results.

  \label{tab:order}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
    \toprule
    Model & Cited first & Shuffled & Cited last \\
    \midrule
    Llama 3.2 3B & 72.3 / 74.1 & 46.3 / 43.1 & 30.0 / 19.9 \\
    Llama 3.1 8B & 73.9 / 69.0 & 67.1 / 60.6 & 89.4 / 85.9 \\
    \midrule
    Qwen3-Embedding-8B & \multicolumn{3}{c}{87.0 / 80.1} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

The 3B model favours early candidates.
Moving the cited papers from first to last costs it 42.3 R-precision points on \emph{random} and 54.2 on \emph{concept} negatives (paired Wilcoxon, $p \le 6 \cdot 10^{-12}$), and its MRR falls from 0.95 and 0.98 to 0.33 and 0.27, below the 0.56 of a random ranking on both benchmarks; R-precision falls below chance only on \emph{concept}.
With the cited papers first, its MRR exceeds the 0.84 that our first evaluation reported, so candidate order alone can account for that result.

The 8B model favours late candidates.
Its R-precision rises by 15.5 and 16.9 points when the cited papers move to the end ($p < 10^{-4}$), and in that order it scores above Qwen3-Embedding on both benchmarks.
The two models fail in different ways.
In shuffled lists, 52.6\% of the 3B model's rankings follow the order they were shown in (Spearman $\rho > 0.8$ between output and input position, over rankings with at least five candidates), against 0.9\% of the 8B model's; the 8B model reverses the shown order more often (10.3\% vs.\ 5.1\%), and half of its rankings (51.5\%) are close to order-independent ($|\rho| \le 0.3$).
Order sensitivity therefore does not disappear with scale: a single fixed order can make the 8B model look better than dense retrieval, as it made the 3B model look better in our first evaluation.

Both effects replicate on human labels (Figure~\ref{fig:order}, right).
The 3B model's loss is 0.253 with the highest-rated papers first, 0.467 shuffled and 0.685 with them last (best- vs.\ worst-first, Holm $p = 1.4 \cdot 10^{-5}$).
The 8B model's is 0.413, 0.388 and 0.253: it again does best when the best papers come last (worst- vs.\ best-first, 43 of 58 researchers, Holm $p = 7.7 \cdot 10^{-5}$), and its rankings correlate negatively with the shown order (mean Kendall $\tau = -0.20$ when shuffled).
The direction of the bias is a property of the model, not of the label source.

\section{RQ2: Which Method Wins Under Controlled Order?}
\label{sec:rq2}

% Embeddings were never tested against each other on human labels; published SPECTER2-max is 0.2215 vs Qwen3 0.2223.

Under shuffled order, Qwen3-Embedding-8B has the best point estimate of the methods we ran on both label sources, 38--43 R-precision points ahead of the 3B listwise reranker and about 20 ahead of the 8B one, while a 22M-parameter MiniLM stays within 4.3 points of it.
On human labels the 8B model improves on the 3B (loss 0.388 vs.\ 0.467) but stays well behind Qwen3-Embedding (0.222; worse for 46 of 58 researchers, Holm $p = 4.6 \cdot 10^{-6}$).
Figure~\ref{fig:agreement} places the eight methods run on both label sources side by side.
The two sources order them similarly (Kendall $\tau = 0.79$; three of 28 pairs swap): the embeddings lead on both, and the listwise Llama models trail on both, the 3B one close to a constant ranking on human labels.
The swaps involve the lexical methods and pointwise Llama, which is fourth on citation labels and sixth on human labels.

\section{RQ3: Does a Second Stage Help?}
\label{sec:rq3}
A second stage does not help: listwise reranking of the embedding's top 10 lowers R-precision by 10--13 points, and the one gain we found, fusing a trained reranker with the embedding score (+3.2 R-precision on citations), does not transfer to human labels.

Table~\ref{tab:rerank} collects every second stage we tried on top of Qwen3-Embedding.
In the cascade on the fixed pools, the embedding's top 10 already holds 93\% and 89\% of the cited papers, so the first stage leaves little to recover; listwise Llama reranking of those ten nonetheless lowers R-precision from 87.0 to 74.0 and from 80.1 to 69.7 ($p \le 3 \cdot 10^{-10}$).
In the full corpus, where the embedding's top 100 is reranked, pointwise Llama alone halves nDCG@10, and fusing its score with the embedding's (weight chosen on dev) changes nothing.
Qwen3-Reranker-4B, a model trained for reranking, ranks the top 100 better than the embedding on its own (within-list AUC 0.824 vs.\ 0.761) but improves nDCG@10 only when fused, by 0.021 points, which does not survive Holm correction; R-precision rises from 25.1 to 28.3 (Holm $p = 0.040$).
On human labels the same reranker is worse than the embedding alone, and a post-hoc fusion with leave-one-researcher-out weights does not beat the embedding either.
The only second-stage gain in this study therefore depends on the label source, and we do not claim it.

\section{RQ4: Do Citation-Graph Signals Add Information?}
\label{sec:rq4}
Citation-graph signals add little on top of a strong dense model: co-citation expansion adds 0.8 recall@100 points (not significant) in the full corpus, and bibliographic coupling improves SPECTER2 but not Qwen3-Embedding on human labels.

\paragraph{Co-citation in the full corpus.}
Qwen3-Embedding finds 64.0\% [58.7, 69.4] of the cited papers in its top 100, so 36\% never reach a second stage.
We replace the bottom 10\% of that list with the papers most often co-cited with its top 5~\citep{small1973cocitation}, following graph-based adaptive retrieval~\citep{macavaney2022gar}, with seed and replacement sizes chosen on dev.
Under the five leakage rules (Section~\ref{sec:citation-labels}) only 54.7\% of test positives have any allowed citing paper; without the conservative date rule this would be 62.9\%, and without the date rule at all 68.1\%, so each rule removes real leaks, such as a 2012 record of a review that holds all ten references of a 2020 query.
Table~\ref{tab:graph} shows the result.
The expansion gains 0.8 points, not significantly, and little more than its controls: the same expansion over a degree-preserving random graph loses 0.5--0.9 points, and over a $k$-nearest-neighbour graph built from the embeddings themselves~\citep{kulkarni2023ladr} it gains 0.3; only 20 of the 548 positives in the expanded lists (3.6\%) came through the graph.
A canary that adds each query's own references to the graph gains 5.8 points, so the method recovers papers when the signal is present; under leakage control, it is not.

\paragraph{Bibliographic coupling on human labels.}
Coupling scores a paper by the references it shares with a researcher's papers~\citep{kessler1963coupling}, a signal that exists as soon as a paper is published.
In an analysis pre-registered before any score was computed, it carries real signal (loss 0.299, better than a constant ranking for 46 of 58 researchers, Holm $p = 4.2 \cdot 10^{-6}$) but trails every embedding, though not significantly.
Fused with an embedding (weights chosen leave-one-researcher-out), it improves SPECTER2 significantly but not Qwen3-Embedding, the model the deployed system uses.
On both label sources, the citation graph adds no significant gain to a strong dense model.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% Label-dependent vs label-independent findings; coupling as a precise but sparse signal; implications for the system.
LLM rerankers should be evaluated under several candidate orders, citation-derived labels should be checked against human judgments, and for our deployed system these results motivate a redesign that removes the LLM stage.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Our human labels are few (58 researchers, almost all in computer science), the rerankers we test are two general Llama models, one trained reranker and one cross-encoder, and pretraining contamination is untested.
